Fun with Filters and Frequencies

2D convolutions, edge detection, and frequency-domain image processing

Amy Wang

Overview

This project is all about filters. The first half builds up the basic tools: writing 2D convolution from scratch, then using small filters to find edges in an image. The second half uses those same tools in the frequency domain for sharpening, hybrid images and multiresolution blending.

Part 1.1 · Convolutions from scratch

Implementing convolution with plain NumPy and checking it against scipy.signal.convolve2d.

Near the edges of the image, the kernel hangs off the side, so I zero-pad the image before convolving. My first version uses four nested loops: two over every output pixel, and two over every entry in the kernel. The second version keeps the two pixel loops but replaces the kernel loops with a single NumPy multiply-and-sum over the window.

def conv4_loops(img, k):
    k = np.flip(k)
    kh, kw = k.shape
    p = np.pad(img, ((kh//2, (kh-1)//2), (kw//2, (kw-1)//2)))
    H, W = img.shape
    out = np.zeros((H, W))
    for i in range(H):
        for j in range(W):
            s = 0.0
            for m in range(kh):
                for n in range(kw):
                    s += p[i+m, j+n] * k[m, n]
            out[i, j] = s
    return out

def conv2_loops(img, k):
    k = np.flip(k)
    kh, kw = k.shape
    p = np.pad(img, ((kh//2, (kh-1)//2), (kw//2, (kw-1)//2)))
    H, W = img.shape
    out = np.zeros((H, W))
    for i in range(H):
        for j in range(W):
            out[i, j] = np.sum(p[i:i+kh, j:j+kw] * k)
    return out

I tested both versions on a grayscale photo of myself, shrunk to a quarter of its size so the four-loop version would finish in a reasonable time.

The box filter blurs the image. Dx picks up vertical edges and Dy picks up horizontal edges. In the derivative images, mid-gray means no change, and light or dark means brightness is increasing or decreasing.

Part 1.2 · Finite difference operator

Using the difference filters to find the edges in the cameraman image.

The finite difference operators Dx = [1, −1] and Dy = [1, −1]T estimate the derivative of the image by subtracting neighboring pixels. In flat areas like the sky, neighboring pixels are about the same, so the output is close to zero. At an edge, brightness jumps, so the output is large. Dx responds to changes going left to right, which shows up on vertical edges, and Dy responds to changes going top to bottom, which shows up on horizontal edges.

To combine the two into one measure of edge strength in any direction, I computed the gradient magnitude at each pixel:

dx  = convolve2d(cam, Dx, mode="same", boundary="symm")
dy  = convolve2d(cam, Dy, mode="same", boundary="symm")
mag = np.sqrt(dx**2 + dy**2)

For this part I switched to scipy.signal.convolve2d for speed. I also switched from zero padding to boundary="symm", which mirrors the image at the borders.

In dI/dx and dI/dy, mid-gray means no change, and light or dark means brightness is increasing or decreasing. The gradient magnitude is always positive, so edges show up bright on black.

Choosing a threshold

To turn the gradient magnitude into a binary edge image, I marked every pixel above a threshold as an edge. There isn't a single right answer here, so I compared 0.1, 0.2, 0.3 and 0.4 by eye:

ThresholdWhat happens
0.1Too noisy. The texture of the grass gets picked up as edges.
0.2Most of the grass is gone and every real edge is still complete, with a few specks left near the bottom.
0.3The grass is basically clean, but the tripod legs start to break up and the buildings mostly disappear.
0.4Too strict. The outline of the man and the tripod fall apart into broken pieces.

I went with 0.25, between the two middle options. It removes most of the grass noise while keeping the outline of the man, the camera and the tripod connected. Going lower brings the grass back, and going higher starts cutting into the real edges.

Part 1.3 · Derivative of Gaussian (DoG) filter

Blurring before taking derivatives to get rid of the noise from Part 1.2.

Building the Gaussian

cv2.getGaussianKernel only returns a 1D Gaussian, a 9×1 column vector. Taking its outer product with its own transpose gives the 9×9 2D Gaussian. I used σ = 1.5 with a 9×9 kernel, since the kernel should be about 6σ wide to hold almost all of the Gaussian's weight.

g = cv.getGaussianKernel(9, 1.5)
G = g @ g.T

Two ways to do it

Method 1: blur, then differentiate. Convolve the image with G, then run the same steps as Part 1.2 on the blurred image.

blur = convolve2d(cam, G, mode="same", boundary="symm")
dx_b = convolve2d(blur, Dx, mode="same", boundary="symm")
dy_b = convolve2d(blur, Dy, mode="same", boundary="symm")
mag_b = np.sqrt(dx_b**2 + dy_b**2)

Method 2: derivative of Gaussian filters. Convolve the Gaussian with Dx and Dy first to make two DoG filters, then apply each one to the image with a single convolution.

DoGx = convolve2d(G, Dx)
DoGy = convolve2d(G, Dy)
dx_d = convolve2d(cam, DoGx, mode="same", boundary="symm")
dy_d = convolve2d(cam, DoGy, mode="same", boundary="symm")
mag_d = np.sqrt(dx_d**2 + dy_d**2)

I used the default "full" mode when building the DoG filters so nothing gets cropped. That's why they come out as 9×10 and 10×9 instead of 9×9.

Results

In the filters, light is positive and dark is negative. DoG x has a bright lobe on the left and a dark lobe on the right, so it responds to vertical edges. DoG y is the same shape turned sideways and responds to horizontal edges. Each filter is a smoothed version of [1, −1].

Do the two methods match?

Yes. The gradient magnitude from blurring and then differentiating (third panel) looks the same as the one from the DoG filters (fourth panel). This is expected because convolution is associative:

(I * G) * Dx = I * (G * Dx)

Blurring and differentiating can be merged into one filter ahead of time, which saves one convolution per direction every time it's applied to an image. The only place the two can differ is within a few pixels of the border, where the mirrored padding gets applied at a different step in each method.

The real edges survive because they're large, consistent changes in brightness that averaging doesn't remove. The edges are also thicker, because the blur spreads each sharp edge out over several pixels.

Part 2.1 · Image sharpening

Making a blurry image look sharper by boosting its high frequencies.

A Gaussian is a low-pass filter: blurring with it keeps the low frequencies and removes the high ones. That means subtracting the blurred image from the original leaves only the high frequencies, the edges and fine detail. An image looks sharper when those high frequencies are stronger, so the idea is to add a little extra of them back in.

Deriving the unsharp mask filter

With image f, Gaussian G and sharpening amount α:

high      = f - f*G
sharpened = f + α(f - f*G)
          = f*((1+α)δ - αG)

Here δ is the unit impulse, a kernel with a 1 in the center and 0 everywhere else, so f*δ = f. Everything collapses into one filter, (1+α)δ − αG, which is the unsharp mask filter. Sharpening then takes a single convolution per color channel. When α = 0 the filter is just δ and the image doesn't change.

def unsharp_filter(ksize, sigma, alpha):
    g = cv.getGaussianKernel(ksize, sigma)
    G = g @ g.T
    delta = np.zeros_like(G)
    delta[ksize//2, ksize//2] = 1
    return (1 + alpha) * delta - alpha * G

def sharpen(img, ksize=9, sigma=1.5, alpha=1.0):
    k = unsharp_filter(ksize, sigma, alpha)
    out = np.stack([convolve2d(img[..., c], k, mode="same", boundary="symm")
                    for c in range(3)], axis=-1)
    return np.clip(out, 0, 1)

The images are in color, so I apply the filter to each channel separately. The result is clipped to [0, 1] because the filter has negative weights and can push pixels slightly out of range.

Results

For the Taj I used a 9×9 Gaussian with σ = 1.5 and tried α = 0, 1, 2 and 4.

The blurred image is the low frequencies, and subtracting it from the original leaves the high frequencies: the edges and the carvings. The high-frequency image is centered around zero, so I multiplied it by 4 and added 0.5 to make it visible, which makes mid-gray mean zero. Adding more of it back gives the sharpened image.

Varying the sharpening amount.

The original is soft, and the carvings on the dome and the arches are hard to make out. At α = 1 and 2 that detail becomes much clearer, and the edges of the minarets and trees look crisper. At α = 4 it's oversharpened: the sky becomes grainy and dark and light halos appear around the edges of the building and trees.

Here the high frequencies are mostly the ripples on the water, the thin reeds and the outlines of the boat and the dock, and those are exactly the parts that get crisper in the sharpened image.

This photo is 4032×3024, so a σ = 1.5 blur barely affects it. I shrank it to a quarter of its size and used a stronger 13×13 Gaussian with σ = 2 and α = 0, 2, 4 and 6. The ripples on the water and the thin reeds get much crisper as α goes up. By α = 6 it's clearly oversharpened: the water looks harsh and noisy, and dark outlines appear around the boat and the people.

Evaluation: blur, then sharpen again

To test how well sharpening works, I took a sharp photo, blurred it on purpose, and then tried to sharpen it back. I shrank my selfie to a quarter of its size, blurred it with a 19×19 Gaussian (σ = 3), and sharpened it with the same Gaussian and α = 2.

The re-sharpened image looks much better than the blurred one. The outlines of the face, eyes and hair, the turtles on the pillow and the plaid on the pants all have clear edges again. But it doesn't match the original:

  • Fine detail doesn't come back. Individual strands of hair, the necklace and the fabric texture are still missing.
  • Edges look artificial. Sharpening makes the remaining edges steeper by adding a light and dark halo along each one, so the face and hair look slightly processed.
  • Higher α is a tradeoff. A larger α makes the edges stronger but also makes the halos and any noise more visible.

Unsharp masking makes an image look sharper by increasing edge contrast, but it doesn't actually recover information the blur destroyed.

Part 2.2 · Hybrid images

Combining two faces into one image that changes depending on how far away you are.

A hybrid image, from Oliva, Torralba and Schyns (SIGGRAPH 2006), mixes the low frequencies of one image with the high frequencies of another. Up close, the high frequencies dominate what we see, so the sharp details of the second image stand out. From far away those fine details are too small to make out and only the smooth, low-frequency image is left.

How it works

  1. Align. The two faces have to line up or the hybrid just looks like two overlapping pictures.
  2. High-pass the first image (im1, the one you see up close) by subtracting its Gaussian blur from itself, the impulse minus a Gaussian from Part 2.1, which keeps only its edges and fine detail.
  3. Low-pass the second image (im2, the one you see from far away) with a Gaussian, which keeps only its overall shapes and shading.
  4. Add the two together.
def low_pass(im, sigma):
    return cv.filter2D(im, -1, gaussian(sigma), borderType=cv.BORDER_REFLECT)

def high_pass(im, sigma):
    return im - low_pass(im, sigma)

def hybrid_image(im1, im2, sigma1, sigma2):
    """High frequencies of im1 (seen up close) + low frequencies of im2 (seen from far)."""
    return np.clip(high_pass(im1, sigma1) + low_pass(im2, sigma2), 0, 1)

Following the starter code, im1 is the image you see up close and sigma1 is its high-pass cutoff, and im2 is the image you see from far away and sigma2 is its low-pass cutoff. I worked in grayscale so the two photos’ very different colors wouldn’t compete with each other.

Frequency analysis

Each panel is the log magnitude of the 2D Fourier transform, np.log(np.abs(np.fft.fftshift(np.fft.fft2(im)))), all shown on the same brightness scale. The center is the lowest frequency and frequency increases toward the edges. The bright cross through the center in every panel comes from the image borders, not from the faces, and the tilted line in Phil’s panels comes from the edge of his rotated photo.

  • The inputs have energy at every frequency, strongest near the center and fading outward, which is typical of photos.
  • Low-passed Dan keeps only a small bright area around the center and everything else goes dark. The Gaussian cut off the high frequencies.
  • High-passed Phil is the opposite: the very center is darker, so the lowest frequencies were removed, while the outer frequencies are unchanged.
  • The hybrid is roughly the two added together: Dan's low frequencies fill the center and Phil's high frequencies fill the rest.

Part 2.3 · Gaussian and Laplacian stacks

Splitting an image into frequency bands, the first step toward multiresolution blending.

A pyramid blurs and then downsamples the image at each level, so each level is half the size of the one before. A stack does the same blurring but never downsamples, so every level stays the same size as the original. I implemented both stacks from scratch, without cv2.pyrDown or skimage.transform.pyramid_gaussian.

Gaussian stack

Level 0 is the original image, and each level after that is the previous level blurred again. I double σ at each level (2, 4, 8, 16, 32), so each level loses about as much detail as a pyramid level at half the size would.

def gaussian_stack(im, levels, sigma=2):
    stack = [im]
    for i in range(1, levels):
        stack.append(blur(stack[-1], sigma * 2 ** (i - 1)))
    return stack

Laplacian stack

Each Laplacian level is the difference between two neighboring Gaussian levels, which holds only the details lost between those two blurs: one band of frequencies. The last level is just the blurriest Gaussian level, so all the levels add back up to exactly the original image.

def laplacian_stack(im, levels, sigma=2):
    g = gaussian_stack(im, levels, sigma)
    return [g[i] - g[i + 1] for i in range(levels - 1)] + [g[-1]]

As a check, I added the 6 Laplacian levels of the apple back together and compared the result to the original. The difference was only floating-point rounding, so the stack loses no information.

The Laplacian levels are centered around zero, so I stretched each one to the full brightness range to make it visible. Mid-gray means zero. Level 0 holds the finest detail, like the texture of the orange peel and the outline of the fruit. Each level after that holds coarser structure, like the highlights and the overall shape, and the last level is just the blurry colors.

Recreating Figure 3.42: the oraple

To recreate the figure from Szeliski, I used a mask that is 1 on the left half (apple) and 0 on the right half (orange) and built a Gaussian stack of the mask too, so it gets smoother at each level. At every level, the apple's Laplacian is weighted by the mask, the orange's by one minus the mask, and the two are added:

left  = GM[i] * LA[i]          # apple, weighted by the mask
right = (1 - GM[i]) * LO[i]    # orange, weighted by the inverse mask
both  = left + right

The rows match the figure in the book. The first three rows show the high-, medium- and low-frequency bands (levels 0, 2 and 4), and the columns show the masked apple, the masked orange and their sum. The key thing to notice is how the seam changes across levels:

  • Level 0: the mask is still nearly a sharp line, so the fine textures meet at a crisp boundary. That's fine, because a sharp seam isn't visible when only tiny details change across it.
  • Levels 2 and 4: the mask has been blurred more and more, so the coarse bands, like the colors and the highlights, fade into each other over a much wider region.
  • Bottom row: adding all the levels together gives the blended oraple. The apple on the left turns smoothly into the orange on the right, with no visible seam, while both halves keep their own sharp textures.

This is the main idea of multiresolution blending: each frequency band is blended over a transition width that matches its size, so fine detail gets a narrow seam and large color regions get a wide one.

Part 2.4 · Multiresolution blending

Using the stacks from Part 2.3 to blend my own images with irregular masks.

The blend works the same way as the oraple. Build a Laplacian stack for each image and a Gaussian stack for the mask, then at every level combine the two Laplacian levels using that level's mask, and add all the levels back together. The mask gets blurrier at each level, so fine details are joined over a narrow seam and large, smooth regions are joined over a wide one.

def blend(im1, im2, mask, levels=6):
    L1, L2 = laplacian_stack(im1, levels), laplacian_stack(im2, levels)
    GM = gaussian_stack(mask, levels)
    return np.clip(sum(GM[i] * L1[i] + (1 - GM[i]) * L2[i] for i in range(levels)), 0, 1)

I made two blends of my own, both with irregular masks, and worked in grayscale for both.

Blend 1: my face on the Mona Lisa

For the first blend, I put my face on the Mona Lisa using an irregular mask. Both images are in grayscale, and I shrank the painting to a quarter of its size (709×1072). To line up the faces, I marked three points in each image, the two eyes and the center of the mouth, and used cv.estimateAffinePartial2D to rotate, scale and shift my selfie so those points land on hers. Using three points instead of two gives a least-squares fit that matches the whole face better.

The aligned selfie looks like a mirrored collage because shrinking and shifting it leaves empty space around the edges, which I filled by reflecting the image. All of that is outside the mask, so none of it ends up in the blend.

Matching brightness

My first attempt looked pasted on. The seam itself was smooth, but my face was clearly brighter and had much more contrast than the painting. Multiresolution blending smooths the transition between two images, but it doesn't change what's inside the mask, so a face with the wrong brightness still stands out. To fix this, before blending I shifted and scaled my face's pixel values so their mean and standard deviation inside the mask match the Mona Lisa's face:

def match_brightness(src, ref, region):
    r = region > 0.5
    return np.clip((src - src[r].mean()) / (src[r].std() + 1e-6) * ref[r].std() + ref[r].mean(), 0, 1)

Blend 2: Twilight's face on Rarity

For the second blend, I put Twilight's face on Rarity, two My Little Pony characters. Both images had transparent backgrounds, so I put them on white first. Then I lined up the heads by rotating, scaling and shifting Twilight so her ear tip and snout tip land on Rarity's, using cv.estimateAffinePartial2D, similar to how I aligned the eyes for the hybrid images in Part 2.2. Aligning by the eyes didn't work because the two characters have different eye spacing relative to their head size. The mask is a tilted ellipse around Rarity's face, so the seam follows a curve instead of a straight line.

Twilight's eyes, bangs and smile sit on Rarity's head, framed by Rarity's own mane and horn. I used 4 levels here instead of 6. The face is small, and with 6 levels the blurriest mask spread far beyond the ellipse, so Twilight's darker coat leaked across Rarity's face as a wide gray halo.

At levels 0 and 1 the mask is still sharp, so only Twilight's fine lines inside the ellipse appear, like her eyelashes and the outlines of her eyes. By level 3 the mask is very blurry, and the coarse shading of the two faces fades together over a wide area.

The most important thing I learned

The most interesting and important part about this project is how important alignment is for image processing. Most of the work was in the setup, not the filtering! Getting a good hybrid or blend depended much more on aligning the faces and matching brightness than on the filter itself. I didn't really get the chance to play around with colors to blend images better so I ended up doing everything greyscale but if I had more time it would be interesting to use colors to blend things more properly.
